# Onboarding the client

**Read `../knowledge-base/what-abm-is.md` first if you have not.** You cannot run an intake
for a play you cannot describe.

---

## The floor is a company name

`PayPal`. Or a URL. Nothing else is required to start.

The governing principle: **ask only what cannot be found, research everything that can.**
Every question below is either blocking because no amount of research can answer it, or
optional because research can. Nothing in the middle.

If the operator answers nothing beyond the name, the skill still runs, and it says out loud
which answers it had to invent from research rather than receive.

---

## Run it as a call, not a form

**A form gets you a bad answer and three days of back and forth.** Clients do not put
effort into forms, the detail comes back thin, you go back with follow-ups, and the first
experience of working together is friction.

On a call they do **less** work and you get **more** detail. And done properly, the call
does the job for you: **the client articulates the targeting, the offer and the copy angles
themselves**, in their own words, which is exactly the material you would otherwise have to
invent and then get corrected on.

The call has two halves. **Their business and their buyer**, then **their offer**,
specifically. The sections below are what to ask; this is how to ask it.

### Part one. The business and the buyer.

- **Who do they help?** Not the vertical, the situation.
- **What result do they get them, and in what time frame?** The time frame is the half
  everyone forgets and it is half the offer.
- **How do they get that result?** The mechanism, in detail. See the warning below.
- **Who are they trying to reach?** Job titles, keywords, the seats to advise on.
- **What tech stack do their clients run?** This is where technographic gates come from.
- **Pull up two or three ideal clients, live, on the call.** Not from memory. The pattern
  in three real accounts beats an hour of describing an ICP.

**Then the question that generates the whole account list:**

> **"What typically happens to someone just before they become your client?"**

An insurer whose clients arrive right after a storm has just told you to build the list
from storm damage. That is the gate, and it came from them.

**Three refinements from the house frameworks, and the third is a hard stop:**

1. **Stack the signal, do not pick one.** Per `buying-signals-intent-not-icp`: first-party
   is their own surface (site visits, free-tool use, content engagement, CRM, inbound
   replies), second-party is ecosystems they are in but do not own, third-party is the open
   web. **First-party converts best and clients never think to mention it.** Ask for it
   explicitly, because they already own it and it is free.
2. **Message follows the buyer's state, not the ICP.** Dormant gets nurture,
   problem-aware gets education, solution-researching gets founder-signed specificity,
   vendor-selecting gets a meeting request. Ask which state the signal implies.
3. **The trigger must BE the intent, not a proxy for it.** Per
   `ccg-offers-niches-campaigns`: *"the public-data trigger must BE the buying intent, not a
   demographic proxy for it."* A plausible-looking precedent is the commonest trap. Storm
   damage genuinely precedes buying insurance. A funding round does not mean they want your
   thing. **If it does not let you say yes or no in sixty seconds, it is a guess.**

### Part two. The offer, and get granular.

- **What exactly do they sell, in their own words?** Write the words down verbatim. This is
  MIRROR's *observe their words, reflect it back* and it is where the copy comes from.
- **What problem are their clients trying to solve?** Their framing, not yours.
- **What questions could we ask a prospect that they would recognise themselves in?** Have
  the client draft them. Those become email openers.
- **What is the result the prospect actually wants?**
- **What makes them different?** Not better. Different.

**Then the one-sentence test, and it is the strongest question on the call:**

> **"If you could only say one sentence to a prospect, what would it be?"**

Push them into this shape:

> *"If we could get you [result] in [time frame] without [pain], would that be worth a
> conversation?"*

Result, time frame, pain, and a question. **Very often that sentence ships as the email
almost unedited.** It is the same shape as the house offer formula,
**Result + Shortcut + Proof + Risk Removal**, and the shortcut is the time frame.

### The warning on the mechanism

You are asking for the mechanism in detail **because it writes the copy**, not because it
goes in the copy.

**Never sell the mechanism.** The house rule is absolute:
*"Sell the connection, not the method."* Not *"we do high-volume cold email"* but *"we can
get you solar jobs"*. Take rates went **up** when the mechanics stopped being explained.
Capture the mechanism at intake for internal use, and keep it out of every ad, page and
email. Same for the intent signal: **never name it back to the prospect.**

### One question the intake did not have, and should

Two-part ICP, part two: **should we take this at all?** Ask for the sellable offer, three or
more case studies, a close rate around 10% or better, and lifetime value that makes a 1:1
build worth the effort. A results filter, not demographics. It is a short conversation and
it is much cheaper than finding out in week six.

## The intake, in the order it should be asked

### A. Who you are, and what you want to promote

| Ask | Blocking? | What it unlocks |
|---|---|---|
| **Your company, and your website** | The floor | Everything. This is the one required input |
| **What do you want to promote?** Which product or offer, not "the company" | No, but ask | GoCardless sells four things. Which one leads changes the gates, the page and the ad. Answer it or the research picks for you |
| **Who is the payer, who is the buyer?** | No | Three different people are involved in most offers and confusing them is the most expensive mistake in this play |
| **What result, in what time frame, without what pain?** | No, but push for it | The one-sentence test above. Often ships as the email |
| **What happens to someone just before they become your client?** | No, but push hardest for it | This is the gate. The account list comes out of the answer |

### B. Who you want to reach. This is the branch.

**Ask first: do you already have a list of companies?**

**If yes** — paste them. Names, or better, URLs. Then say this plainly:

> **A supplied list is a hypothesis, not an instruction.** It gets screened against the
> same four gates as a derived one, and some of it will fail.

That is not pedantry. On the GoCardless run, two of six supplied accounts failed gate 4
**after** their pages had been built and their ad sets created. Both had to be unpublished
and both left orphaned ad sets behind. Screen first, build second, every time.

**If no** — the gates get derived from the offer and the list gets built. That is
`sops/02-layers-and-account-list.md`, and it is the harder, better path, because a derived
list carries its own reason for existing.

Then, whichever branch:

| Ask | Blocking? | What it unlocks |
|---|---|---|
| **Do you know your buying committee, or should we find it?** | No | If they know it, the seats are given and only the names are missing. If not, the seats get derived from the offer first. `../knowledge-base/buying-committee-ladder.md` |
| **Is any account off limits?** An existing customer, a partner, live litigation, an ex-employee's new employer | No, but always ask | This one is only ever known by them, and getting it wrong is embarrassing in a way research cannot save you from |
| **Any geography imposed?** | No | Sets the location filter and the entity you will be reading filings from |

### C. What you can hand over. All optional, all accelerate.

Ask for these as a list and take whatever comes. Silence here is fine.

- A **deck or one-pager** for the offer
- A **price sheet**, or the shape of the commercial model
- **Case studies or proof** you are allowed to quote, and who is allowed to be named
- A **past campaign** that worked, or one that failed and why
- Anything from the **CRM**: closed-won patterns, closed-lost reasons, churned accounts
- **Anything you know about the buyer that is not public.** This is the highest-value
  answer in section C and almost nobody volunteers it

**An attached document is a cross-check, never a substitute for the research.** It tells
you what they believe about themselves. The research tells you what a stranger can verify.
When the two disagree, that gap is usually the most interesting thing in the engagement.

### C1b. The escape hatch. Ask it early, and be willing to hear no.

Everything after this point costs hours. **One question decides whether they are worth spending**,
and it is the one an eager operator skips because the answer might be inconvenient:

> **"If we find nothing publishable about these accounts, is this engagement still worth running?"**

Three answers, and each one changes the job rather than ending it.

| Their answer | What it means | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| "No, the evidence is the whole point" | The engagement lives or dies on gate 3 | **Run gate 3 on three accounts before anything else.** If it fails, say so on day one, not in week three |
| "Yes, we mostly want the pages and the ads" | They are buying production, not research | Say plainly that the ads will be well made and thinly argued, price it as production, and **do not let the deliverable imply research that is not there** |
| "We already know who to target, we just cannot reach them" | A handed-over list | **This is a different play.** The account list is not derived, so most of this skill does not apply. Say so rather than performing the steps |

**Also ask, in the same breath, whether this is a live engagement or a demonstration.** A demo
never activates, never needs conversions and never needs a real budget, so three of the blockers
that stall a live build simply do not apply. Knowing which one you are in changes what "finished"
means, and it is cheap to ask and expensive to assume.

### C2. The one question that decides where the evidence comes from

Ask it out loud, and write the answer into the client profile as an output in its own right:

> **"When one of these accounts has the problem you solve, who finds out about it, and are
> they obliged to write it down anywhere?"**

The answer names the **ladder**, and the ladder decides everything Step 8 does. Three answers
seen so far:

| Their answer | The ladder | Where the evidence lives |
|---|---|---|
| "Their investors and their auditor" | UK corporate disclosure | results, transcripts, filed accounts |
| "It goes out to tender, it is public" | UK public procurement | Find a Tender, the Contracts Finder bulk archive |
| "Nobody, it is internal" | **none yet** | say so, and go find one before building a list |

**A third answer is a real answer.** It means the account list cannot be derived from public
evidence yet, and that is a finding worth telling the client on the call rather than three
weeks later. It does not end the engagement, it changes what you sell them.

The failure this question exists to prevent: the skill knew one ladder and quietly assumed
every client was on it. The client after that one was a public body, and its evidence touched
**none** of the four corporate rungs.

### D. The six blocking questions

None of these can be researched. All six must be answered before anything is built.

1. **Which layer?** Are we pitching this company, or building the campaign this company
   would run against its own accounts? See `sops/02-layers-and-account-list.md`.
   *This question cost three days on the GoCardless build because nobody asked it.*
2. **Live or demo?** If demo, nothing ever runs, no commercial terms are needed, and the
   output is the walkthrough in `sops/10-demonstration-and-ledgers.md`.
3. **Which ad account, and which LinkedIn page?** Sets the org URN, the account guard and
   the geo restrictions.
4. **Is there a budget, and is activation on the table at all?** **Never default a budget.**
   The upstream kit's own recorded failure: *"Assumed the daily budget. FIX: Phase 0, ask, never
   default."*
5. **Have you applied for LinkedIn Marketing API access yet?** Not *do you have it*, but
   **have you applied**. See below.
6. **What can a stranger say yes to, in one step, without trusting you?** This is the
   cold-traffic offer and **it is the one that was missed**. Everything this play builds is
   aimed at people who have never heard of the client, and a page that ends in "speak to
   sales" is a warm ask. Push until you have all four: the **specific** outcome, the **finite**
   time frame and scope, why it is **new money or new information** rather than an improvement
   on something they already have, and how the **risk** is handled. Then ask the question
   nobody asks: **what receives the yes?** A form, an upload, an address. If the answer is "the
   website", there is no offer yet. Read `../knowledge-base/cold-traffic-and-the-offer.md`
   before you run this question, and run `offer-temperature-check` on the answer.

**On a demonstration, question 6 has no client to answer it, so the operator answers it.**
Infer a cold offer from what the client already publishes, present it on the page **as a real
offer**, and disclose the inference in the footer disclaimer and in the handover. A specimen
stamp on the call to action makes the page honest and unbookable, which is a real failure from
the Revolut run. The disclosure belongs where the audience is the operator and the client, not
where the audience is the buyer.

### D2. The API question, and helping them answer it

Everything this skill builds runs on the LinkedIn Marketing API, and **approval is manual
with no published timeline. Four weeks at best, around four months on average.** So this is
a lead time, not a task, and it belongs on day one rather than on build day.

Three possible answers and what each triggers:

| Answer | What to do |
|---|---|
| **Yes, we have it** | Check it rather than believe it. Token TTL with the Token Inspector, which permissions the app actually holds, and **whether the ad account is mapped to the app** under Products → View Ad Accounts. An unmapped account is the most common reason a valid-looking token returns nothing |
| **No, not applied** | If the engagement is live, **start the application today, in parallel with everything else.** Nothing upstream is blocked by it. Walk them through it: `../knowledge-base/linkedin-api-access.md` has the exact path |
| **We won't be getting it** | Say the two honest options out loud. Run it as a demonstration, which needs no API at all except the final build step. Or run it on our ad account, which changes who the advertiser is and is a layer question |

**Tell them the thing that saves them months: Development tier is enough.** The Advertising
API's Development tier allows POST against up to five ad accounts, and a 1:1 ABM run uses
one. Standard tier removes the account limit, requires a screen recording of the platform
creating and optimising campaigns, and **is not needed for this play**. People wait for
access they did not need.

Helping here is not a favour. If they cannot get access, half of what we build cannot run,
and finding that out in week six is worse for both sides than finding it out in hour one.

### E. The second channel. Optional, and worth offering.

**By the end of this play you will hold a named buying committee**: person, seat, why they
are on it, and a LinkedIn URL. That list was built to aim the ads. **It is also, row for
row, an email list.**

So offer it at intake, plainly, as an option and not as an upsell:

> *"The same list that aims the ads can carry an email to the same named people. Do you
> want the second channel, or ads only?"*

**Why it is worth doing, and it is not a preference.** The upstream kit's own funnel puts **seven to
ten touches across channels** before a sales conversation, and has sales moving **within 48
hours of an engagement spike**. An ad-only play delivers the top of the funnel and stops.
The email is not a parallel campaign; **it is the touch that lands on someone who has
already seen you four times.** That is the whole argument, and it only works in that order.

Ads first, email second. Never the reverse.

**What it actually costs, said before they agree:**

| | Cost |
|---|---|
| The committee, named, with LinkedIn URLs | Free from filings and company pages, except the champion seat |
| The champion seat | ~0.5 credits per person previewed. Names and titles, no emails |
| **The email addresses** | **1 credit per address found, free when none is found** |
| Sending | Existing infrastructure. See the `email-deliverability-audit` skill before the first send |

**What to ask if they say yes:**

- Is there a **domain we can send from**, or do we warm one? A cold domain is weeks.
- Who **owns the reply**? An email that lands with nobody to answer it is worse than no email.
- Is any of this list **already in their sequences**? Double-touching a live prospect from
  two systems is how a good account gets burned.
- Are there **contacts they are contractually barred** from mailing?

**If they say no, say what they are leaving.** They keep the ads, they lose the ability to
reach the exact named person the evidence was built about, and the play stops at the top of
the funnel. That is a legitimate choice. It should just be a choice, not an omission.

### F. The shortest opening that works

This is what a good one actually looks like, and it answers most of the intake in four
lines without being asked:

> *"We're Company X, here's our site. We sell [product]. I want to target these five
> companies, here are their URLs. Here's our price sheet. I don't know who the buyer is
> inside them."*

From that: section A is answered, section B branches to **supplied list, screen it**,
section C has a price sheet, and the committee is a known unknown. Section D is still
missing, and those five are one message. Ask the API question in the same breath, because
it is the only one with a multi-week lead time.

## The research. Mandatory whatever was answered.

Produce a client profile with seven outputs. This runs even when every question above was
answered, because the answers are what the client believes and these are what can be shown.

| # | Output | Standard |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **What they sell, in their own product names** | Read their site live. For GoCardless: Success+, Pay by Bank, Instant Bank Pay, Protect+. Not "payments" |
| 2 | **The mechanism in one sentence** | A stranger has to understand it |
| 3 | **Who pays whom** | The payer, the biller, the buyer. **Three different people.** Confusing them killed four of five creatives on the first GoCardless pass |
| 4 | **What they publish as proof** | Quoted whole. Never a regex capture |
| 5 | **Do they already have a cold offer of their own?** | GoCardless did, the Success+ recovery calculator. Knowing that changes the page |
| 6 | **Brand: palette and logo** | Scraped and looked at, never guessed |
| 7 | **The four gates, derived for this client** | The bridge to the account list. See below |

## Output 7 is what makes this skill transferable

**The gates are not hardcoded. They are derived from output 1.**

For GoCardless they came out as: recurring collection at scale in sterling, 300+ addressable
UK staff, listed or bonded so a narrative exists, and a collection surface visible in the
accounts. **That fourth gate exists only because GoCardless sells collection.**

For PayPal the same research would produce different gates: cross-border transaction volume,
checkout abandonment, currency-conversion fee exposure. Same shape, different content.

So onboarding does not merely gather context. **It writes the filter that selects the
account list** — and it is also the filter a *supplied* list gets screened against.

## When the client is the one being pitched (layer 1)

They are not in the room to answer. The operator fills the intake, sections B and C are
almost always empty, and the skill must say so rather than waiting for answers that will
never arrive.

| API access treated as a build-day task rather than a multi-week lead time | Question D5, at intake. Ask whether they have **applied**, not whether they have it |

## The gate that closes this step

The profile is complete when all seven outputs are written, each of the four gates is
stated in one sentence specific to this client, the API question has a dated answer rather
than an assumption, **and the cold-traffic offer is written down with the thing that receives
the yes named**. **If a gate could apply to any company, the
product research is not finished.**
