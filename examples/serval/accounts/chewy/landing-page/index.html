What makes Chewy different from a company of the same size is not the number. It is the shape of it. A workforce that carries a large part-time and fulfilment population does not sit still. People arrive in waves, and they leave in waves, and the IT consequence of both is identical and unavoidable.

A new starter needs an account, a group, a device, a shared drive and access to three systems they have never seen. A leaver needs every one of those taken away, on the day, or it becomes a security finding. Neither task is hard. Both need a person today, and there are two of them for every single hire.

Why it does not get better

A ticket system routes. It has never resolved

This is worth being plain about, because it is not a criticism of ServiceNow. It does exactly what it was built to do.

A service desk takes a request, puts it in a queue, decides who should see it, and tracks how long it sits there. Every one of those verbs is about moving the request. Not one is about finishing it. At the end of every well-routed, well-categorised, well-tracked ticket there is still a person who has to go and click the thing.

So the work scales with headcount and the capacity scales with hiring, and when hiring slows, the gap does not close. It widens quietly, one request at a time, until the queue is the job.

Automation inside a ticket system does not fix it either, because the automation is a rule somebody has to write, test and maintain, and the person who would write it is the same person who is behind on the queue. That is why most of these projects stall after the top three request types. The fourth one is never worth the afternoon.

What changes

An agent that finishes the request instead of filing it

Serval puts two agents where the queue is. One sits in Slack and takes the request from the employee, then goes and does it: creates the group, grants the access, provisions the new starter, revokes the leaver. Not a suggested article. The action, completed, with the employee told it is done.

The second is for your team. It builds the automations from a sentence typed in plain English, so the fourth request type and the fortieth stop being an afternoon each. That is the part that decides whether this keeps going after the first three, and it is the part a rules engine inside a ticket system has never solved.

For a workforce that flexes, the onboarding and offboarding half is where this lands first. One of Serval's customers describes onboarding thousands of contractors with no manual setup at all, which is the same shape of problem as a hiring wave.

The first needs nothing from you but ten lines of typing, and you can do it without telling anyone. That is deliberate. Everything anyone has asked

Including the awkward ones

We already have automation in ServiceNow.

You do, and it almost certainly covers your top three request types. The question is what happened to the fourth. Rule-based automation stalls where the effort to write the rule exceeds the pain of doing the task once, which is most of the queue.

Our volume is seasonal. It is only a problem at peak.

That is the strongest argument for it rather than against it. A queue that triples for six weeks is exactly the thing you cannot solve by hiring, because you cannot hire and train an IT person for six weeks.

We are mid-renewal. Wrong time.

It is the right time for the opposite reason. A renewal is when somebody asks what the licences are for, and the only useful answer is a number showing what the queue costs to run.

Compliance will not allow an agent to grant access.

Reasonable, and it is the question that should be asked first. Permissions are scoped per action rather than granted wholesale, and the sandbox route exists so your security engineer watches exactly what it does before anything is connected.

What happens when it gets something wrong.

It hands the request to a person, the way the queue works now. The failure mode of an agent that cannot finish something is the status quo, not an outage.

We run two desks for a reason. Engineering will not move to IT's tool.

They are not being asked to. The agent sits in Slack, where both populations already are, and the request never has to reach either portal.

How is this different from the AI features our current vendor ships.

Those suggest an article or draft a reply. The difference is whether the thing gets done. Cribl's director of IT support put the gap at fifteen minutes into a first call, which is his description and not ours.

Our fulfilment staff are not at a desk. Slack does not reach them.

Then the fulfilment population is out of scope for the Slack route on day one and the corporate population is not. Saying so is more useful than pretending otherwise.
